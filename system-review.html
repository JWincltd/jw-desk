01

The pipeline, in plain terms

What the desk actually does every morning

Well before the open, one data call sees the entire liquid US market. A floor of ~400 analyst agents reads only the names that moved overnight; most moves are dismissed as noise on purpose. A chief agent synthesizes the survivors into a regime read and a short list of genuine calls. Those calls become the three cards on the board — and from that moment they are tracked, not just shown: every card is replayed against real daily prices as simulated P&L, so the desk's record is measured, never asserted.

The same discipline runs underneath: ideas the desk rejected are graded too (so we learn what our filters cost us), benchmark agents score whether the firm beats a coin-flip, and the whole engine now watches itself for silent degradation. The guiding rule everywhere is anti-fabrication — if a number isn't real, it isn't shown.

The work since the last review

What we sharpened, and why

Coverage & calendar

Self-maintaining macro calendar

Live

Why: a desk that misses CPI or FOMC is useless. It now pulls the authoritative economic calendar from the Fed's own data feed every week — 47 dates through year-end, automatically, no hand-entry, never wiped on failure.

Periodic reports (weekly → annual)

Live

Why: to see the trend, not just today. Each report has a market half (are we making money) and a system half (is the machine improving) — tied to the prior report, with a built-in devil's-advocate that argues against our own direction.

The options cards — a complete trade

Exit target from support/resistance

Live

Why: the cards showed where to enter but not where to exit. Now each scans ~2 years of price history for the real ceiling (calls) or floor (puts) above/below the trade — a genuine profit target, not a guess.

Odds of reaching the exit

Live

Why: a target without a probability is half an answer. The odds are drawn from how often this stock actually made a move that size — and now conditioned on the real setup (past breakouts on volume), not random days.

Grading matches the plan

Live

Why: the record must measure the trade we actually published. The simulator now exits at the shown support/resistance target — the track record and the card are finally the same trade.

Earnings-in-window flag

Live

Why: an option carried across its own earnings report is an event bet, not a technical setup. Any play with a report inside its life gets a loud red warning — never a silent trade into a landmine.

Position sizing — Full / Half

Live

Why: edge per idea is worthless without "how much." Each card is tagged Full or Half size — halved on lean conviction, or when two plays share a sector so we don't double one bet unknowingly.

Live contract detail

Pending data

Why: a play should name the exact contract. Strike, expiry date, premium range and implied volatility pull from the live options chain — the code is built and waiting on one data subscription (see decisions).

Rigor & hygiene

Full audit + pre-mortem

Done

Why: to find what we didn't know we didn't know. Read every module, stress-tested the engine, and found 4 real bugs — including one that silently rejected every play on a $1,000+ stock (Eli Lilly is in the book). All fixed with permanent tests.

Model-quality tripwire

Live

Why: the whole floor runs on one AI model that a vendor could quietly swap or degrade — and nothing would notice. A daily fingerprint of the engine's output now alerts on any abrupt shift.

Spread-friction metric

Dormant

Why: a paper edge can vanish into real option spreads. We record the round-trip spread per play — the honest "does the edge survive costs" read. We deliberately did not fake a net-of-cost return we can't yet compute correctly.

Dead-code removal + polish

Done

Why: less surface, fewer places to break. Removed ~2,400 lines of unreachable legacy code, standardized the thesis cards, and sorted them by conviction so the strongest ideas lead.

04

The trust story

How the desk keeps itself honest

This is the part worth dwelling on together — it's what separates this from a stock-picking newsletter.

  • The counterfactual ledger. Every idea the desk rejected is graded by the same simulator, so each filter gets a verdict: is it saving us from losers, or costing us winners?
  • Benchmark scorekeepers. Mechanical agents (buy-and-hold, momentum, random) run through the identical grader, so "does the firm beat a coin-flip?" is always answerable.
  • Conservative grading. Ambiguity always grades against us — a bar that could be a win or a loss is scored a loss. The record is never flattered.
  • "Calibrating," not "hit rate." Below a real sample the board says calibrating and refuses to quote a rate off noise. We hold ourselves to that for the first quarter.
  • The tripwire. A silent model downgrade is the one failure that could rot everything quietly — it's now watched.

Where we are honestly blind

Blind spots — stated plainly

None of these are hidden failures; they're the known edges of what the system can see today. Naming them is the point.

  • Execution reality. Simulated P&L assumes fills a real options buyer might not get. We now record the spread (the honest cost read); a true net-of-cost return needs the next item.
  • Option-leg P&L. We grade the underlying move, not the option's own price path (leverage, time decay). This is the single biggest gap between the record and reality.
  • Post-entry management. The simulator enters once and exits once — no scaling out, no trailing stops, no time-stops that a real desk uses.
  • Small-sample noise. At a few plays a week, the first quarter of "hit rate" is statistically thin. The system says so; we must resist tuning on it.
  • Single-vendor dependency. One AI provider, one price feed. The tripwire now catches a silent model change, but concentration remains.

To decide together

The calls in front of us

  1. Subscribe to live options data? Decision The exact strike, expiry, premium and IV are built and dormant, waiting on a Polygon Options plan (~$29/mo entry tier is enough, since the brief is built pre-open). Without it, cards show a "feed not connected" note and everything else still works.
  2. Build the option-leg simulator? The one project that closes the biggest measurement gap — grading the option's own P&L (with spread and decay), so "is this a real edge?" becomes measured, not believed. Best done right after the options data is live.
  3. Add correlation-limited sizing? We already cluster the book by correlation; nothing consumes it for sizing yet. A rule that trims size when a new play crowds an existing position is cheap and high-value.
  4. How hard do we push short strategies? Only the deep-support-break short has cleared the out-of-sample bar, and it's paper-traded. The question is patience vs. capital — keep it on paper until the record is undeniable, or graduate a small live allocation.
